Pulled three quotes from two different sources. My responses are beneath the quotes.

Programming Design Systems Link

  • Q1: Design systems offer a different way of thinking, where the designer is forced to consider many scenarios and constraints instead of relying on a one-off design process.
  • R1: I have worked at companies that did not establish a design system and continued to desgin one-off pieces of work. It can be an extremely stressful situation to be in when deadlines loom. I have also worked at companies that have a design system in place, that work across different design teams. Spending time and resources to establish a design system is worth it in the long run; it lessens the chances of going off brand and/or website platforms looking different from one team to the next team's work.
  • Q2: It is important to know these rules when working with color, as they enable designers to create graphics that are accessible to a majority of users. However, to master the art of color combination, designers also have to know how color is used in different cultures and contexts, observe current trends in the arts, and develop a personal style based on this knowledge.
  • R2: I learned about color therory in undergraduate school and continue to use it in my design practice. I also learned about how color is used in different cultures and finding out what the colors symbolize. Take for instance the Philippine flag: Yellow is used for the sun and three stars. It carries the meaning of freedom, unity, democracy, and sovereignty; Blue is the top half of the flag and it represents peace, justice, sacrifice, and truth; Red is on the bottom half and it signifies bravery, courage, and patriotism; White is used as the background of the triangle which represents liberty, equality, and fraternity. When the blue is on top, it symbolizes that the country is at peace; when the flag is raised upside down with the red on top, the country is at war. The color of the flag is also used on traditional Filipino clothing and modern Filipino fashion.
  • Q3: Although many of the web technologies we use today existed in some form, web designers were limited to a few basic techniques when creating websites.
  • R3: If you grew up using AOL and dial-up, there's a good chance you had a geocities website. It's one of the first places I learned the basics of HTML, play with graphical images and edit journal entries. It was a fun time but when looking back there were limitations. What I find interesting about this quote is that despite the limitations, people found ways to be creative and move the technology forward.

What Do Prototypes Prototype? Link

  • Q1: It is up to the designer to prepare an audience for viewing a prototype. Prototypes themselves do not necessarily communicate their purpose. It is especially important to clarify what is and what is not addressed by a prototype when presenting it to any audience beyond the immediate design team
  • R1: At my previous job, my team created prototypes of the user flow by connecting the corresponding screens. We knew what the prototype and screens were about but the stakeholders and cross-functional teams may or may not. Instead of having them make assumptions, my team would present our prototypes, clarify what is and isn't addressed, communicate the purpose and how it integrates with the larger system as a whole. After the bulk of the presentation was walked through, time after would be spent answering questions and receiving feedback for further iterations.
  • Q2: Look and feel prototypes are built primarily to explore and demonstrate options for the concrete experience of an artifact. They simulate what it would be like to look at and interact with, without necessarily investigating the role it would play in the user's life or how it would be made to work. Designers make such prototypes to visualize different look and feel possibilities for themselves and their design teams. They ask users to interact with them to see how the look and feel could be improved. They also use them to give members of their supporting organization a concrete sense of what the future artifact will be like.
  • R2: I've been a user tester for design products and tested out digital prototypes. I've found it fun to interact with prototypes because despite it not being "real" or in the hands of the consumer, it was great to be able to give user feedback about the prototype. It's even better seeing the launched product implement a suggestion a user has made as it gives a sense of validation.
  • Q3: The pizza box was a very efficient prototype. Spending virtually no time building it or considering options, the students got useful feedback on a basic design question—what physical form would be best for the user. From what they learned in their simple field test, they knew immediately that they should try to think beyond standard rectilinear notebook computer forms. They began to consider many different options including designing the computer to feel more like a soft shoulder bag.
  • R3: I pulled this quote as this gave me a better view of what prototyping is about. How can you not love the pizza box prototype. No time was spent building it as it existed already. The fact that visualized a pizza box as a possible shape for a portable computer, that is known to us as laptops, is simply genius.

These two articles were very fun to read. I learned a lot about programming and prototyping from reading these articles.
